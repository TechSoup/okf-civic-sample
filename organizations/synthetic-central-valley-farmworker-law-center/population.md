# Who the Law Center serves

**⚠ Synthetic — fabricated data.**

The Law Center serves **farmworkers and their households** in Fresno and three adjacent counties — roughly **900 matters a year**, opened for something over 700 distinct households. Clients are predominantly Spanish-speaking, with a significant **Mixtec and [[Triqui]]**-speaking minority, and include both settled residents and seasonal and **H-2A** workers who are in the region for part of a year. *(**derived**, simulated — counts from grant reporting, not from case files, which were not examined.)*

## What is known about this population, and what deliberately isn't

The organization holds detailed information about its clients — it has to, in order to represent them. That information is **privileged**, and it does not appear in this bundle in any form. What follows is the aggregate picture the organization publishes.

Three groups with materially different needs:

**Settled resident farmworkers** — the largest share. Wage claims, housing conditions, workplace safety. Often long-term relationships with the organization across multiple matters over years.

**Seasonal and H-2A workers** — present for part of the year, sometimes housed by the employer they have a complaint against, and frequently gone before a matter concludes. The organization's hardest population to serve for a structural reason: **the legal timeline outlasts the person's presence in the county.** A wage claim can take eighteen months; the worker may leave in October.

**Households in removal proceedings** — the smallest group and the most acute. The removal-defense practice is chronically oversubscribed, and the organization turns away more of these than it accepts, which it describes as the hardest recurring decision it makes.

## The intake problem, which is really a trust problem

**People do not report their employer to a law office.** Not because they don't want help, but because the calculus is bad: the employer may also be the landlord, the last person to complain was let go, and a precarious immigration status makes any contact with an institution feel expensive.

So intake happens where trust already exists. An advocate at a mobile pantry tailgate — see [synthetic-Valle Verde Food Network](../synthetic-valle-verde-food-network/README.md) — gets more usable intakes in a morning than the office phone line gets in a week. Know-your-rights sessions in a community hall produce cases weeks later, when someone who sat quietly at the back calls.

Anything designed for this organization has to start there. A web intake form is not a small improvement over the current channel; it is a channel this population largely will not use, for reasons that are entirely rational.

## Language

**Spanish** is the working language of most of the practice. **Mixtec and Triqui** speakers are served through interpreters, and in this context those are primarily **oral** languages with variation between communities — the same constraint the Law Center's partner organization documents in [its own population file](../synthetic-valle-verde-food-network/population.md). Legal work makes it harder: there is no reliable written Mixtec rendering of a wage-claim form, and interpretation for a legal matter requires an interpreter who can carry legal meaning, not just words.

The organization maintains a paid interpretation line for languages it cannot staff, and it is a real budget item.
