# Who Prawo i Schronienie serves

**⚠ Synthetic — fabricated data.**

The foundation serves **refugees, applicants for international protection, and migrants** in Poland — roughly **2,400 matters a year**, most in Warsaw and Mazowieckie but with national reach for some categories. *(**derived**, simulated — from programme reporting, not case files, which were not examined.)*

## Three populations with three legal situations, and the distinction is load-bearing

Anything reading this bundle should not flatten these into "refugees." In Polish and EU law they are different statuses with different rights, procedures, and timelines, and the organization's work is largely about which one a person is in.

**Beneficiaries of temporary protection** — predominantly people displaced from Ukraine from 2022 onward, under the EU temporary protection framework. The largest group by volume. Their legal position is comparatively secure and their problems are administrative: status renewals, documentation, access to services, employment, school enrollment, and the accumulating question of what happens when a temporary framework has lasted four years.

**Applicants for international protection** — the asylum procedure proper. Far smaller in number, far more intensive per matter, and where the organization's litigation capacity is spent. Includes people who arrived via the Belarus border situation, whose cases are legally and practically the hardest the organization takes.

**Migrant workers** — third-country nationals on residence and work permits, with employment-rights problems: unpaid wages, permits tied to a single employer, and the specific vulnerability that comes from a legal right to remain that depends on continued employment. The organization's employment work overlaps considerably with what [the California farmworker bundle](../synthetic-central-valley-farmworker-law-center/population.md) describes, in a different legal frame.

## What GDPR changes about a population file

**The most useful structural observation in this bundle.** In the US bundles, what an organization records about the people it serves is governed by policy, funder requirements, and judgement. Here it is governed by **law that gives the person rights over it**.

Concretely:

- **Every category of data needs a lawful basis** before it is collected. Not a justification written afterwards — a basis identified in advance and recorded.
- **Data subjects have enforceable rights**: access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability. A client can ask what the organization holds and get an answer.
- **Special category data** — which includes health data, religious belief, ethnic origin, and sexual orientation, all of which appear routinely in asylum matters — has a higher bar again.
- **Data minimization is a legal requirement**, not a good practice. Collecting a field because it might be useful later is not permitted.

The practical effect is that this organization's answer to "why don't you collect X" is often **"because we have no lawful basis to"** — a categorically different answer from [Valle Verde's](../synthetic-valle-verde-food-network/population.md) protective policy choice or [Frogtown Table's](../synthetic-frogtown-community-table/population.md). Same shape of absence in the data; three different reasons; and the collection now has all three.

**Anything scoring completeness across these bundles should be able to tell them apart.** It cannot from the data alone.

## Language

Working languages: **Polish**, **[[Ukrainian]]**, **Russian**, **English**, and — for asylum matters — a long tail requiring interpretation, including Arabic, Kurdish, Tigrinya, Dari, and Pashto.

The organization staffs Polish, Ukrainian, and Russian internally, which is why its temporary-protection work runs at volume. Everything else needs a sworn or specialist interpreter, which is a real budget line and a real bottleneck in the asylum procedure — a hearing does not happen without one.
